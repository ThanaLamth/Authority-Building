- Name the teams, the investors, the VC backers, the governance structures
- End with the unresolved question: what happens when an autonomous agent makes a trade that loses a user's funds — who is liable?

### Blueprint
**H1:** Who Controls AI Agent Crypto Projects? The Power Map Behind the 2026 AI x Crypto Boom

**Opening sentence (CCpress style — named actor, immediate conflict):**
"ai16z, the AI agent fund that reached $500M in assets under autonomous management by early 2026, is controlled by a DAO — but the DAO's founding team retains administrative override keys."

**Central tension:**
Autonomous AI agents that hold and move cryptocurrency are a new class of financial actor. They are not regulated as exchanges, not licensed as brokerages, and not governed by any consumer protection framework. The projects behind them are — in most cases — VC-backed teams operating through token governance structures.

**Power map structure (CCpress signature):**

Section 1 — The actors:
- Infrastructure layer: who built the agent frameworks (ElizaOS, Olas Network, Fetch.ai)
- Token layer: who issued the speculative tokens (ai16z, VIRTUAL, GOAT)
- VC layer: who funded them and what exit incentives exist
- User layer: who is actually using these agents and what they risk

Section 2 — The accountability gap:
- If an AI agent drains a wallet: what recourse does the user have?
- Governance token holders: nominal control, practical powerlessness
- Admin override keys: who holds them, what are the conditions for use

Section 3 — Regulatory vacuum:
- CFTC: AI agents executing commodity trades — regulated or not?
- SEC: AI agent tokens as unregistered securities — ongoing risk
- EU MiCA: does not cover autonomous agents explicitly — policy gap noted

Section 4 — The taxonomy (required for accountability journalism):
```
Type 1: Infrastructure (ElizaOS, Olas) — tools, not funds
Type 2: Agent tokens (ai16z, VIRTUAL) — speculative, high concentration risk
Type 3: Autonomous protocols (Fetch.ai, Bittensor) — most complex liability structure
```

**Timeline block:** Key events in AI agent crypto — launch dates, token events, regulatory mentions

**"Key players" sidebar:** VC firms, founding teams, regulatory bodies watching this space

**Unresolved question (required CCpress ending):**
"The question regulators have not answered — and the industry has not asked loudly enough — is this: when an autonomous agent executes a trade using a governance structure that no user actually controls, who is responsible for the outcome?"

### Blueprint
**H1:** Tokenized Stock Perpetuals: Who Is Exposed When the SEC Acts?

**Opening sentence (CCpress style — stakes first):**
"Several DeFi protocols allow users to trade perpetual contracts tracking Apple, Tesla, and S&P 500 prices on-chain, without registering with the SEC. The SEC has not acted yet. The platforms are not waiting for permission."

**Central tension:**
Tokenized stock perpetuals are on-chain synthetic derivatives that track real equity prices via oracle feed. They are not stocks. They are not registered with the SEC. They are not operated by licensed brokerages. And they are being used by US-adjacent traders who have no regulatory protection if a platform fails.

**Investigative structure:**

Section 1 — How they work (brief, functional — CCpress explains mechanisms only enough to establish the stakes):
- Oracle feed tracks real stock price (Chainlink, Pyth)
- User holds a synthetic derivative — not the stock, not a token of the company
- Liquidation risk: oracle failure = cascade liquidation with no recourse

Section 2 — Who is operating these platforms:
- dYdX: historically offered stock perps, delisted some under regulatory pressure
- Synthetix: sSPY, sAAPL — synthetic stocks on Optimism
- Drift Protocol (Solana): equity exposure
- Kwenta: Synthetix front-end
- GMX: does it offer stock perps? (check current status)
- Name the teams, the VC backers, the governance structures

Section 3 — The SEC question:
- Howey Test applied to synthetic stock perpetuals: are they securities?
- SEC's position on similar products (enforcement history with crypto derivatives)
- What "enforcement risk" actually means for users vs platform operators
- Geographic exposure: US persons trading on these platforms

Section 4 — Oracle risk as accountability issue:
- If the Chainlink feed for AAPL fails during high volatility, users can be mass-liquidated
- Who is responsible? The oracle provider? The protocol? The governance token holders?
- Precedent: Synthetix oracle exploit history

Section 5 — What a regulatory action looks like:
- SEC cease-and-desist scenario: platform shuts, US users lose open positions
- CFTC parallel action (perpetuals may be CFTC jurisdiction, not SEC)
- User recourse: none, in practice

**Timeline block:**
- Key regulatory actions on synthetic derivatives (2021-2026)
- dYdX delisting decisions and stated reasons
- Any SEC or CFTC communications on synthetic equities

**"Key players" sidebar:**
- Platforms: dYdX, Synthetix, Drift, Kwenta
