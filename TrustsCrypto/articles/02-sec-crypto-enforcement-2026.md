The Securities and Exchange Commission filed a complaint against Coinbase Global, Inc. in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York on June 6, 2023, per the court filing published at sec.gov under case number 1:23-cv-04738. The complaint alleges Coinbase has operated as an unregistered national securities exchange, an unregistered broker, and an unregistered clearing agency.

## What the SEC Filed Against Coinbase and When

The complaint was filed the day after the SEC filed a separate action against Binance on June 5, 2023. The two cases are procedurally separate and involve different legal theories in parts.

The SEC's complaint against Coinbase names 13 digital assets it alleges are securities traded on the platform: SOL, ADA, MATIC, FIL, SAND, AXS, CHZ, FLOW, ICP, NEAR, VGX, DASH, and NEXO. The complaint does not allege that Coinbase itself issued these tokens. The theory is that by offering, selling, and facilitating trading in these assets without registering as a national securities exchange, Coinbase violated Sections 5 and 6 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

The complaint additionally covers Coinbase's staking service, alleging it constitutes an unregistered offer and sale of securities under Section 5 of the Securities Act of 1933.

Coinbase is contesting the case. It has not entered a settlement or consent order. The company filed a motion to dismiss in August 2023. Judge Katherine Polk Failla denied the motion to dismiss in March 2024, finding the SEC had adequately alleged that several of the named tokens could be securities under the Howey test. That ruling does not constitute a finding that any token is a security. It means the case proceeds to the merits stage.

There is no parallel Department of Justice action against Coinbase in this matter. There is no parallel CFTC action against Coinbase in this matter at the time of writing.

## Why the Charges Are Significant: Legal Theory and Precedent at Stake

The core legal question is whether the Howey test applies to digital assets sold on secondary markets without privity with the original issuer. The SEC's position is that a token can be a security even when purchased on an exchange from an unknown counterparty, if the original sale involved a common enterprise and expectation of profit from others' efforts.

Coinbase's defense argues that secondary market transactions lack the investment contract structure the Howey test requires, and that applying securities law to these assets without Congressional action violates the major questions doctrine. That doctrine holds that agencies cannot claim authority over questions of major economic and political significance without clear Congressional authorization.

The motion to dismiss denial means Judge Failla found the SEC's theory plausible enough to proceed, not that she accepted it. The distinction between "plausible enough to litigate" and "proven at trial" is material for assessing the outcome.

If the SEC prevails on the exchange registration theory, every US platform trading the named assets -- and potentially others sharing their characteristics -- would face a retroactive registration obligation or would need to delist those assets to avoid liability. That outcome would have structural consequences for the secondary market for those tokens in the United States.

## Which Products, Users, and Markets Are Directly Named in the Filing

The 13 named tokens are the products most directly affected by the exchange registration theory. The staking service allegation separately implicates users who earned rewards through Coinbase's Ethereum staking product.

The complaint does not name Bitcoin or Ether as securities. The SEC has stated separately that it does not consider Bitcoin a security. The Ether question was addressed in a separate context when the SEC approved spot Ether ETF applications in May 2024.

Users who purchased the 13 named tokens on Coinbase between the alleged violation dates and the filing are not named as defendants and are not subject to enforcement. However, the outcome of the case affects the legal status of those assets on US platforms going forward.

## What Has Not Been Determined: Pending Court Decisions and Unresolved Claims

The trial on the merits had not been scheduled as of this article's last review date. The court set a discovery schedule in 2024, and subsequent procedural steps had not been fully resolved.

The court has not made a finding that any of the 13 named tokens is a security. The denial of the motion to dismiss means only that the SEC's theory is legally sufficient to go forward, not that Coinbase violated the law.

Whether the staking service constitutes an unregistered securities offering has not been decided. Coinbase suspended its staking service for US customers in February 2023 following a separate Kraken settlement on a related theory, but the complaint's allegations regarding prior staking activity remain in the case.

Pending confirmation: at the time of writing, no trial date had been set and the case was in the discovery phase. Any summary judgment motion by either party, and the court's ruling on it, will be the next major procedural milestone.

## What Affected Parties and the Industry Should Watch

A summary judgment ruling by either party would be the first substantive legal determination of Coinbase's liability on the merits. If the SEC moves for summary judgment on any of the 13 token claims and succeeds, that finding would directly define the securities status of those assets in the US market.

The outcome of the motion on the exchange registration theory carries the most structural weight. A finding that Coinbase operated as an unregistered exchange would require the company to either register with the SEC or restructure which assets it offers to US users.

The staking service allegation's resolution matters separately for any platform offering staking yields to US retail users, regardless of the Coinbase outcome.

Congressional action on crypto asset regulation remains possible and could moot portions of the case. Several bills proposing a framework that allocates authority between the SEC and CFTC over digital assets have been introduced in Congress. None had been enacted at time of writing.
