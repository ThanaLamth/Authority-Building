## 10 Key Crypto Terms Defined (2026 Edition)

These definitions reflect current usage as of July 2026. Where a term has evolved from its original meaning, the current usage is defined first, followed by a note on the original.

---

### Market Cap

Market cap in crypto is the total market value of a token's circulating supply, calculated as: current price multiplied by circulating supply.

It is not a measure of money invested in the asset. It is a snapshot of what the market would value all circulating tokens at the current price, not what it would cost to buy all tokens at that price.

Bitcoin's market cap as of July 2026 exceeds $1.9 trillion, according to CoinGecko. That figure reflects price multiplied by roughly 19.7 million BTC in circulation, not the total dollars that entered Bitcoin markets.

**What traders often get wrong:** Comparing market caps across different supply schedules is misleading without adjusting for emission rates. A token with 1 billion circulating supply and a $1 price has the same market cap as a token with 10 million supply and a $100 price, but the inflation trajectories may be completely different.

**Why this matters for TrustsCrypto readers:** Every ranking in our [Top 10 Crypto by Market Cap](/top-10-crypto-by-market-cap-2026) article uses circulating market cap, not fully diluted valuation (FDV). Those are different numbers. FDV includes all tokens that will ever exist at the current price.

### DeFi

DeFi (Decentralized Finance) refers to financial services built on public blockchains, where smart contracts execute transactions without a centralized intermediary.

DeFi protocols include lending (Aave, Compound), decentralized exchanges (Uniswap, Curve), liquid staking (Lido), and derivatives platforms (dYdX, GMX). Each of these runs code on-chain that executes autonomously based on predefined rules.

The term is often used loosely to mean "anything crypto-native that earns yield." That usage is imprecise. A centralized exchange offering yield on deposits is not DeFi, even if it pays in crypto tokens.

**What traders often get wrong:** "DeFi" does not mean safe or decentralized by default. Smart contract risk, oracle manipulation, and governance attacks are endemic to the category. Aave has been secured through governance and audits for years, but newer protocols in the same category carry significantly higher risk.

### Staking

Staking is the act of locking a proof-of-stake token in a validator or protocol contract to earn rewards, typically denominated in the same token.

There are three distinct staking contexts that are frequently conflated:

1. **Native staking:** Running a validator node (Ethereum requires 32 ETH). Rewards come from protocol issuance and transaction fees.
2. **Liquid staking:** Depositing ETH with Lido or Rocket Pool and receiving a yield-bearing token (stETH, rETH) in return. No 32 ETH minimum.
3. **Exchange staking:** Locking tokens on a centralized exchange in exchange for an interest payment. This is not on-chain staking; it is a custodial yield product.

The important distinction: native and liquid staking involve on-chain contract interactions you control. Exchange staking involves trusting the exchange with custody.

**What traders often get wrong:** Exchange "staking" on Binance or Coinbase is a centralized yield product. If the exchange is insolvent, staked tokens are at risk as counterparty liabilities. That is not a risk that exists in native Ethereum staking, where the 32 ETH is controlled by the validator's own keys.

**Lock-up disclosures:** Some staking products impose lock-up periods. Ethereum's withdrawal queue can take days to weeks depending on network congestion. Always verify lock-up terms before committing funds.

### Layer 2

Layer 2 (L2) refers to a blockchain network that processes transactions off the main chain (Layer 1) and posts compressed proofs or transaction data back to the main chain for finality.

Ethereum is the dominant Layer 1 in this context. Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, and zkSync are Ethereum Layer 2 networks. They inherit Ethereum's security model but execute transactions faster and at lower cost.

There are two main technical architectures: optimistic rollups (Arbitrum, Optimism, Base) and zero-knowledge rollups (zkSync Era, Starknet, Polygon zkEVM). The distinction matters because optimistic rollups have a 7-day challenge period for withdrawals, while ZK rollups can finalize faster.

**What traders often get wrong:** Not all "L2s" are equivalent. Some projects use the term loosely to describe sidechains (Polygon PoS) or independent chains (BSC) that do not post proofs to Ethereum. Those are not Layer 2 networks by the technical definition.

**Current status as of July 2026:** Arbitrum and Base lead Ethereum L2 TVL, collectively holding over $18 billion according to L2Beat's public data. These figures are updated in real time and should be verified directly at L2Beat before citing.

### Gas Fee

A gas fee is the cost paid to validators or miners to include a transaction in a block on a proof-of-work or proof-of-stake blockchain.

On Ethereum, gas fees are denominated in gwei (a unit of ETH) and vary with network congestion. On Bitcoin, fees are denominated in satoshis per virtual byte. On Solana, base fees are fixed and very low (sub-cent), with priority fees added during congestion.

Gas fees are not optional. Every on-chain transaction requires a fee, regardless of transaction value. Sending $10 of ETH during high congestion can cost more in gas than the ETH being sent.

**What traders often get wrong:** Gas fees are paid to the network, not to the exchange or protocol you are interacting with. Adding liquidity to Uniswap costs gas regardless of Uniswap's own fee structure. Those are separate costs.

**Why Layer 2 matters here:** One reason Layer 2 networks like Arbitrum exist is to reduce gas fees. Transactions that cost $20 on Ethereum mainnet can cost under $0.10 on Arbitrum or Base.

### Self-Custody

Self-custody means holding the private keys to a crypto wallet yourself, without relying on a third party like an exchange or custodian.

The phrase "not your keys, not your coins" expresses the core risk: if an exchange holds your crypto, you are a creditor of that exchange. If the exchange fails (as FTX did in November 2022), your crypto becomes part of the bankruptcy estate.

Self-custody requires managing a seed phrase: a 12 or 24-word recovery phrase that can regenerate the private key if the device is lost. Losing the seed phrase with no backup means permanent loss of access to the wallet.

**What traders often get wrong:** Self-custody is not the same as security. A self-custodied wallet with a seed phrase stored in a plain text file on a cloud drive is less secure than an exchange account with 2FA. Self-custody shifts the risk from counterparty default to personal operational security.

The self-custody principle is a recurring discussion in [widely-shared DYOR resource threads on Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/CryptoCurrency/comments/osmb00/several_resources_and_websites_to_help_you_dyor/) — the community consistently links hardware wallet guides alongside exchange risk explainers as paired reading.

**Two primary self-custody methods:** Software wallets (MetaMask, Exodus) run on internet-connected devices. Hardware wallets (Ledger, Trezor, Coldcard) keep private keys on an offline device. For large holdings, hardware wallets are strongly preferred for security. ### Yield Farming

Yield farming is the practice of deploying crypto assets across DeFi protocols to earn the highest possible return, often by moving funds between protocols as incentive rates change.

Yield farming emerged with Compound's COMP token launch in June 2020, which rewarded liquidity providers with governance tokens in addition to interest. The term now covers a wide range of strategies including providing liquidity to DEX pools, lending on money markets, and participating in protocol incentive programs.

The yield sources in farming include: trading fees from liquidity pools, borrowing interest from lending markets, and token emissions from protocol incentive programs. Token emissions are inflationary by nature and can depress the value of the reward token over time.

**What traders often get wrong:** High APY numbers in yield farming often reflect token emission rates, not underlying economic yield. A 200% APY denominated in a new protocol's governance token that loses 90% of its value leaves the farmer with a net loss.

**Specific risks:** Impermanent loss (when providing liquidity to a DEX pool and the price ratio of the two assets changes), smart contract risk, and rug pull risk (protocol team exits with pooled funds). All three risks are distinct and compounding. Evaluated across specific platforms in our [Top 7 DeFi Passive Income Platforms](/top-defi-passive-income-platforms-2026).

### DAO

A DAO (Decentralized Autonomous Organization) is an organization governed by smart contracts, where decision-making authority is distributed across token holders who vote on proposals.

DAOs govern a significant portion of the DeFi ecosystem. MakerDAO governs the DAI stablecoin's risk parameters. Uniswap DAO controls the UNI treasury and fee switch. Compound DAO manages protocol upgrades and interest rate models.

Voting power in most DAOs is proportional to token holdings, which means large holders (often venture capital funds and early team allocations) have outsized governance influence. This is a known limitation of the one-token-one-vote model.

**What traders often get wrong:** DAO governance is not immune to centralization. In several major DAOs, fewer than 10 addresses control more than 50% of voting power, according to governance analytics published by Boardroom. Decentralization is a spectrum, not a binary.

**YMYL note for this article:** DAO participation can involve financial risk. Voting on parameter changes (collateralization ratios, fee settings) that are subsequently exploited can result in protocol losses that affect token holders. Understand the governance model before participating.

### RWA (Real-World Asset)

RWA (Real-World Asset) in crypto refers to a blockchain-based representation of an asset that exists off-chain, such as a government bond, private loan, real estate deed, invoice, or commodity.

The tokenized RWA market reached $15 billion in TVL as of Q2 2026, with US Treasuries making up the largest share, according to RWA.xyz. The category grew because on-chain Treasury yields (4-5%) were significantly higher than most native DeFi yields in a normalized rate environment.

An RWA token is not the same as the underlying asset. It is a legal claim, structured through an SPV, a registered fund, or a trust, that the blockchain token represents. The enforceability of that claim depends on legal documentation, jurisdiction, and counterparty solvency.

**What traders often get wrong:** RWA tokens are not risk-free just because the underlying asset is a government bond. The bridge between the on-chain token and the off-chain asset introduces counterparty risk, legal risk, and operational risk that do not exist in the underlying instrument itself.

### On-Chain

"On-chain" refers to data or activity that is recorded directly on a blockchain ledger, verifiable by anyone with access to the chain's data.

On-chain data includes: wallet balances, transaction history, smart contract interactions, token transfers, and protocol TVL. These are facts derivable from the blockchain state without trusting any third party.

The contrast is "off-chain": data that exists in company databases, legal documents, exchange order books, or other systems not recorded on a public blockchain. Exchange trading volume, for example, is reported by exchanges but is not on-chain unless it occurs on a DEX.

**Why this matters for research:** TrustsCrypto separates on-chain evidence from off-chain claims throughout our coverage. An on-chain TVL figure (verifiable at DeFiLlama) is different from a protocol's self-reported AUM. The distinction tells you how much to trust the number.

The distinction between on-chain verifiable data and self-reported metrics surfaces regularly in [crypto tools discussions on Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/CryptoCurrency/comments/18huo4f/what_tools_do_you_use/) — traders asking what tools to trust most consistently point to Glassnode, DeFiLlama, and Etherscan as the on-chain tier versus exchange APIs.

**Relevant tools for on-chain verification:** Etherscan (Ethereum), Solscan (Solana), Arkham Intelligence (entity tagging), Glassnode (aggregated on-chain metrics), Nansen (wallet profiling). These are the primary tools we reference when citing on-chain data in TrustsCrypto articles.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between market cap and fully diluted valuation?**
Market cap uses circulating supply. Fully diluted valuation (FDV) uses total maximum supply, including tokens not yet in circulation. FDV represents the hypothetical market cap if all tokens were circulating at the current price. Projects with large unreleased supply have a significantly higher FDV than market cap.

**Is self-custody always safer than using an exchange?**
Not necessarily. Self-custody eliminates exchange counterparty risk but introduces personal operational risk: losing the seed phrase, device theft, phishing attacks, or malware. For most users, a combination of a hardware wallet for long-term holdings and a reputable exchange for active trading is a reasonable balance.

**What is the risk in yield farming that is not obvious?**
The least obvious risk is smart contract upgrade risk. A protocol can issue a governance proposal to upgrade a contract and, if passed, change the behavior of a pool or vault you have funds in. Reading the governance history of any protocol before depositing funds is a step most retail yield farmers skip.

**What distinguishes an on-chain metric from a self-reported metric?**
An on-chain metric can be independently verified by querying the blockchain directly or using a trusted indexer like DeFiLlama or Glassnode. A self-reported metric is published by the protocol or company and cannot be independently confirmed without access to their internal systems. When TrustsCrypto cites a number, we specify which type it is.
