The Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act (GENIUS Act), S. 394, was introduced in the United States Senate on February 4, 2025, by Senator Bill Hagerty (R-TN), per the bill text published at congress.gov. The bill proposes a federal licensing framework for payment stablecoin issuers operating in the United States. The word "proposes" is appropriate here because the bill's final enacted form, if any, may differ materially from the introduced text.

## What the Bill Proposes: Key Provisions and Requirements

The GENIUS Act defines a "payment stablecoin" as a digital asset that is issued by a permitted payment stablecoin issuer, is designed to be used as a means of payment or settlement, has a fixed nominal value denominated in dollars or another specified currency, and is redeemable on demand.

The bill establishes two primary licensing pathways. Large issuers, defined as those with more than USD 10 billion in outstanding stablecoin value, would be federally chartered and supervised by the Federal Reserve or the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, depending on their charter type. Smaller issuers could operate under state money transmission or state stablecoin licensing frameworks, provided those state frameworks meet minimum federal standards established in the bill.

Reserve requirements under the proposed bill require 1:1 backing with permissible reserve assets. The bill text lists permissible reserves as: U.S. coins and currency, demand deposits at insured depository institutions, short-term U.S. Treasury bills, and instruments issued by the Federal Reserve. Algorithmic stablecoins that use an endogenous digital asset to maintain their peg are addressed separately: the bill as introduced includes a two-year moratorium on new issuance of endogenous algorithmic stablecoins, with regulatory review during that period.

The bill requires monthly public disclosure of reserve composition, certified by the issuer and, for large issuers, subject to audit by a registered public accounting firm.

## Why These Provisions Matter for Issuers, Banks, and Users

The licensing pathway for non-bank issuers is the provision most significant for the existing stablecoin market. Circle, Paxos, and similar companies that issue stablecoins outside of a bank charter would need to obtain a federal nonbank payment stablecoin issuer license from the OCC or operate under a qualifying state framework. The bill does not require a full bank charter to issue stablecoins, which is a departure from proposals in earlier legislative sessions.

The reserve asset list is more permissive than the European MiCA framework in some respects: the U.S. bill permits short-term Treasury bills as eligible reserves without a minimum deposit floor at credit institutions. Under MiCA, at minimum 30% of EMT reserves must be held as deposits in credit institutions.

The Fed's role in supervising large issuers means the central bank would have direct oversight of stablecoin issuers above the USD 10 billion threshold. Existing large issuers -- specifically Tether (USDT) and Circle (USDC) -- would both exceed that threshold based on outstanding supply at the time of introduction.

## Which Entities and Products Fall Within the Bill's Scope

The bill's definition of payment stablecoin is written to cover dollar-pegged tokens designed for payments and settlement. Tokens designed primarily for governance, speculation, or lending collateral without a payment purpose may fall outside the definition depending on their design.

Foreign issuers serving U.S. users are addressed in the bill: non-U.S. issuers that make payment stablecoins available to U.S. persons must comply with the bill's requirements or face restrictions on U.S. market access. The specific enforcement mechanism for foreign issuers is one of the provisions that remained under negotiation at time of writing.

The bill explicitly does not apply to bank deposits, money market funds, or securities, preserving the boundary between stablecoins and existing regulated financial products.

## What Remains Contested, Unresolved, or Subject to Amendment

The bill passed the Senate Banking Committee with bipartisan support in March 2025. Its subsequent path through the full Senate, and any House companion bill, involved additional negotiations. The bill's treatment of Big Tech companies as potential stablecoin issuers was contested, with some senators proposing amendments to restrict or prohibit commercial technology companies from issuing payment stablecoins directly.

The preemption clause -- whether the federal framework would preempt state money transmission laws for issuers that obtain a federal license -- remained under negotiation at time of writing. States with existing crypto licensing frameworks, including New York, expressed concern about loss of supervisory authority.

The moratorium on algorithmic stablecoins was contested by some market participants who argued the two-year period was too short to enable responsible regulatory development. Pending confirmation: the final enacted text, if any, may differ from the bill as introduced. The bill's status at congress.gov should be checked for current procedural stage.

## What Issuers and the Industry Should Watch as the Bill Progresses

The bill's current stage at congress.gov is the authoritative source for procedural status. The stages to watch are: Senate floor vote, House companion bill introduction and committee action, any conference process to reconcile differences, and Presidential action.

Treasury, Federal Reserve, and OCC comment letters submitted during the legislative process carry weight because those agencies will write the implementing regulations if the bill is enacted. Those letters are available on agency websites and through the Senate Banking Committee's public record.

Issuers currently operating under state frameworks should assess whether their state's licensing regime would qualify as a "qualifying state framework" under the bill's minimum standards provisions. That determination matters for whether they would need to seek a new federal license or could continue under state regulation.
